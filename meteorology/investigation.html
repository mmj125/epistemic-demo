Energy & Earth

How much energy reaches Earth, what happens to it, and how do we know? Work through the physics, verify it against real satellite measurements, then run your own investigation.

Energy From the Sun

Consider Earth as a system. Energy constantly enters it from the Sun and leaves it back out to space, carried entirely by light — different wavelengths of the electromagnetic spectrum, from gamma rays to radio waves. Shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon; longer wavelengths carry less.

Every object radiates light based on its temperature — hotter objects radiate more total energy, and radiate it at shorter (higher-energy) wavelengths. This is why a stovetop element glows visibly red-hot before it gets hot enough to glow white. Try it below: the Sun's surface is about 5,778 K; Earth's surface is about 288 K.

Radiation vs. Wavelength

Wavelength is plotted on a log scale, 0.1 to 100 microns. Each curve is normalized to its own peak so the Sun and Earth can be compared on one chart, even though the Sun radiates far more total energy.

What you should notice: the Sun's curve peaks in the visible band (~0.5 microns) — that's not a coincidence, our eyes evolved to see where the Sun's output is strongest. Earth's much cooler surface peaks far out in the infrared (~10 microns), radiating a type of light we can't see but can feel as heat.

Two Students Disagree

Student 1: "I think the Sun gives off most of its energy at ultraviolet wavelengths, because ultraviolet light is more intense than visible light, and you always hear about UV light causing sunburns."
Student 2: "Even though UV photons are more energetic than visible photons, the Sun simply gives off fewer ultraviolet photons, and gives off way more visible and infrared photons. So I think these longer-wavelength photons account for most of the energy coming from the Sun."
Student 2 is closer. A photon's energy depends on its wavelength, but the total energy in a band depends on both photon energy and how many photons are emitted there. The Sun's blackbody curve peaks in the visible band and has already dropped off sharply by the time you reach UV — UV photons are individually more energetic, but there are far fewer of them, so they carry a small share of the Sun's total output.
Student 1: "The Sun mainly gives off visible light, and so does Earth's surface, because I can see it during the daytime."
Student 2: "But that's just reflected sunlight. Earth's surface is much cooler than the Sun and mostly gives off energy closer to the kind that our bodies give off — infrared light. I'm not sure, but I think the surface probably radiates infrared light during both the daytime and the nighttime based upon its temperature."
Student 2 is right. What you see coming off Earth's surface in daylight is reflected visible sunlight, not light the surface itself is emitting. Every object radiates based on its own temperature, day or night — Earth's surface is about 288 K, cool enough that its own emission is almost entirely infrared, invisible to human eyes, but present around the clock.

Naming the Flows

Energy entering and leaving Earth is measured in watts per square meter (W/m²), and split into named quantities depending on its source and direction. These are the same names you'll see in Tab 3's real satellite data.

ISRIncoming Shortwave Radiation — the energy from the Sun that initially enters the Earth system.
RSRReflected Shortwave Radiation — the amount of ISR reflected away without ever being absorbed.
ASRAbsorbed Shortwave Radiation — the amount of ISR that stays within the Earth system (ASR = ISR − RSR).
OLROutgoing Longwave Radiation — radiation Earth emits back out to space.
DLRDownward Longwave Radiation — radiation absorbed by the atmosphere and re-emitted back down toward the surface.
NARNet Absorbed Radiation — the net energy entering the Earth system, accounting for what's absorbed and what's outgoing (NAR = ASR − OLR).
AlbedoThe ratio of reflected to incoming shortwave radiation (Albedo = RSR ÷ ISR) — a surface's reflectivity, from 0 (absorbs everything) to 1 (reflects everything).

One Number Hides a Lot

A single global average is real, but it flattens away everything that varies by location and season. Sea ice is a good place to see why that matters: bright ice reflects far more sunlight than dark open ocean does, meaning the exact same amount of incoming sunlight (ISR) produces very different albedo depending on what's actually on the ground — or the water.

The ice-albedo feedback

As sea ice retreats, it exposes darker open ocean where there used to be bright, reflective ice. That lowers the surface's albedo, so more incoming sunlight gets absorbed instead of reflected, which warms the water and melts more ice. It's a self-reinforcing loop, not a one-time effect.

Well-established mechanism. Check it yourself: Arctic region, siconc and albedo, Trend Map.

The two poles don't behave the same way

Arctic sea ice has declined steadily since satellite records began in the late 1970s. Antarctic sea ice, though, stayed roughly stable, even slightly increased, for decades, before an unusual sharp drop starting around 2016. The Arctic is an ocean mostly surrounded by land; Antarctica is a continent surrounded by open ocean, and Southern Ocean wind and circulation patterns drive Antarctic sea ice in ways that don't mirror the Arctic's.

The observed difference is well-documented. Exactly why the recent Antarctic drop happened when and how it did is still actively researched. Check it yourself: compare siconc trend maps for the Arctic and Antarctic presets.

Polar amplification

The Arctic has warmed several times faster than the global average over the past few decades. The ice-albedo feedback is a major reason, but it's not the only one, atmospheric and ocean circulation changes and how heat gets transported toward the poles all play a role too.

The pattern itself is robustly observed. How much each individual driver contributes is still being worked out. Check it yourself: compare t2m trends for the Arctic preset against Global.

Clouds don't just do one thing

Clouds reflect incoming sunlight back to space, a cooling effect, but they also trap outgoing heat the way greenhouse gases do, a warming effect. Which one wins depends on the cloud's type, altitude, and location, low thick clouds tend to cool, high thin clouds tend to warm.

This net balance, and especially how it might shift as the climate warms, is genuinely one of the biggest open questions in climate science right now, not settled fact. Check it yourself: cld and cre (cloud radiative effect).

Land heats up faster than ocean

Given the same energy input, land surfaces warm up more, and faster, than ocean surfaces do. Water has a much higher heat capacity and loses energy efficiently through evaporation, so it absorbs and stores more energy per degree of warming; land has no such buffer.

Well-established, and a good one to start with if you want something more straightforwardly mechanistic. Check it yourself: t2m, comparing a land-heavy region against an ocean-heavy one.
