Primate Locomotion Investigation

Instructor Guide &
Resource Library

Everything you need to run the Primate Locomotion Investigation in your classroom. This is an in-class, instructor-led activity — not a self-paced course. The platform is designed to be used alongside your instruction, not instead of it.

About this investigation

Students are told a fossilized primate specimen has been found and they must determine how it moved and how big it was — using only the bones and a comparative dataset of 478 modern primates. There is no single correct answer. Students must synthesize four lines of quantitative evidence and construct a defensible written argument under genuine uncertainty.

Core pedagogical approach

Teach a thing, do a thing. Each module follows the same pattern: you introduce a concept through direct instruction and class discussion, then students apply it in the platform using real data. The platform handles computation and visualization so students can focus on scientific reasoning rather than software.

Productive ambiguity is intentional. No single metric resolves the investigation. Students who expect a matching game will be surprised. That surprise is the learning.

A note on the "right answer"

The mystery specimens are chosen so that no single analytical method produces a confident identification. The intermembral index will cluster near species with different locomotor strategies. Body mass estimates will rule out some possibilities but not others. The olecranon index and bone geometry each add information without resolving the question. This is deliberate — it models how actual paleontological inference works.

The reveal at the end of the investigation is gated behind argument submission specifically so students cannot skip to the answer. When the identity is revealed, the follow-up question — "What does this tell you about the limits of your evidence?" — is where the deepest learning happens.

Instructional Sequence
Four modules, flexible pacing
1
Limb Proportions & Locomotion
Teach · Morphosource · Platform Tab 1
You teach
Locomotor diversity and why it exists (form follows function, natural selection). The bones of interest: humerus, radius, femur, tibia. How to compare limb proportions across body sizes using the intermembral index. Students observe locomotion videos and form hypotheses before seeing any data.
Students do
Measure limb bone lengths for their mystery specimen in Morphosource. Enter measurements into the platform. Explore scatter plots and IMI distributions across 478 comparative specimens. Begin noticing where their specimen clusters — and what that does and doesn't tell them.
Slide deck
Covers tetrapod origins, locomotor diversity, bones of interest, the IMI formula, and measurement protocol. Includes activity prompts (Parts A–D) for class discussion and hypothesis formation.

2
Body Mass & Biomechanics
Teach · Morphosource · Platform Tabs 2–3
You teach
Body mass estimation from skeletal measurements and why size matters for locomotion. Levers, mechanical advantage, and how the elbow joint functions as a third-class lever. The olecranon process as the triceps insertion point — a longer olecranon means more force, less speed.
Students do
Measure the femoral head diameter in Morphosource and estimate body mass using five taxon-specific regression equations. Measure olecranon length and ulnar diaphyseal length to calculate OUI. Plot their OUI against reference ranges from the literature and interpret what the mechanical advantage implies about forelimb use.
Slide decks
Two decks for this module: the Biomechanics lecture covers lever classes, mechanical advantage, and body joints. The Post-Discussion deck covers ulnar morphology across locomotor groups with comparative data from published literature.

3
Cross-Sectional Geometry
Teach · ImageJ · Platform Tab 4
You teach
Bone as a living tissue that responds to mechanical loading. Wolff's Law and bone plasticity: habitual movement leaves a structural record in cortical bone. Cortical area as a proxy for overall bone robusticity. Imax/Imin as a measure of loading directionality — circular cross-sections indicate multidirectional loading, elliptical sections indicate planar loading.
Students do
Open CT cross-section images of the femur and humerus midshaft in ImageJ (browser-based). Run the MomentMacro to extract cortical area, Imax, Imin, Imax/Imin, and polar moment J. Upload the log files to the platform. Compare femur and humerus values across species using log-scale box plots and a humerus vs. femur J scatter plot with a 1:1 reference line.
Slide deck & files
The CSG lecture covers bone biology, cross-sectional properties, and the MomentMacro workflow. Students also need the MomentMacro .txt file and their specimen's CT images (distributed separately).

⬇ MomentMacro .txt
4
Argument Construction & Reveal
Platform Tab 5 · Class Discussion
You teach
Scientific argumentation: what makes a claim defensible, how evidence is weighed against competing interpretations, and why acknowledging conflicting evidence strengthens rather than weakens an argument. Review the structure-function connection across all four metrics students have collected.
Students do
Write a four-part argument: claim, supporting evidence (with specific numbers from all four analyses), conflicting evidence, and adaptation/natural selection reasoning. Submit for AI formative feedback. Revise. Then and only then, reveal the specimen identity. Class discussion follows: what did we get right, where were we wrong, and what does that tell us about the limits of skeletal evidence?
Discussion questions
  • Which metric was most useful? Which was most misleading?
  • What additional evidence would have helped?
  • How does this relate to how paleontologists actually work?
  • What would change if the specimen were from a different habitat?
Student Entry Points
Four mystery specimens

Each specimen has a different field context and produces different analytical tensions. Assign different specimens to different student groups for richer class discussion — groups will have partially overlapping but not identical results.

Instructor Reference
Specimen identities & key tensions
Specimen Identity Key pedagogical tension
MS-11 Alouatta seniculus
Red Howler Monkey		 IMI falls in mid-range arboreal quadruped zone shared by several species. Body mass (~5–10 kg) is the key discriminator — students must use size estimates to narrow the field.
MS-12 Presbytis obscura
Dusky Leaf Monkey		 IMI ~90–91 sits in dense cercopithecoid overlap zone. Students cannot distinguish arboreal from terrestrial quadrupeds on IMI alone — must integrate habitat context and bone geometry.
MS-13 Indri indri
Indri (largest living lemur)		 IMI ~66.5 clusters near humans and sifaka — hindlimb dominance from vertical clinging, not bipedalism. Field context (West Africa) is deliberately incorrect; Indri is Malagasy. Two misdirections to navigate.
MS-14 Erythrocebus patas
Patas Monkey		 IMI overlaps with arboreal cercopithecoids despite highly terrestrial, cursorial lifestyle. Open savanna habitat is the essential additional evidence. Fastest primate on the ground (~55 km/h).
