Why Triangles

For a joint to stay put, every push and pull on it — from the members connected to it and any load — has to cancel out exactly. That's Newton's first law: no net force, no motion. A triangle gives every joint enough directions of support to always find that balance. Some other shapes don't. Load Path

This is the fully-triangulated version of the last structure — one diagonal per bay, nothing redundant left to remove. The weight at the tip has to travel all the way to the two pins before it's actually supported. Every member along the way ends up either stretched (tension, red) or squished (compression, blue) as it carries its share. And by Newton's third law, whatever total force the structure pushes into the pins, the pins push back exactly as hard — that's what "supported" means. Iteration Log

Before you design anything yourself, get a feel for how the scoring works. These are four designs a different student already tried — real deflection, cost, and force concentration numbers, just not yours. Force concentration is the largest force carried by any single member: low means the load is spread out, high means one member is doing most of the work — the member (or connector) most likely to be the one that fails first in a physical build. There's no single "best" design here — each of the three sliders has its own favorite among these four. Drag one all the way up at a time and watch the winner change.

Predict vs. Build

Once your physical model is built and tested, come back here and compare what the design tool predicted to what you actually measured. This isn't really a test of whether you built it correctly — it's a test of the model itself: how well does a 2D simplification at half the real load actually predict a real 3D structure? They won't match exactly — that's expected, and worth thinking about why, not something to explain away.

Why might these differ? A few real possibilities — this isn't multiple choice, you can think it's more than one:

  • Real K'NEX joints have some play in them — they're not the perfectly rigid pins the model assumes.
  • The model is one face at half load, not the full 3D object — the cross-bracing does something the 2D model can't see.
  • Measuring a physical deflection by hand has its own error.

Design Argument

Make the case for your final design. In real engineering, an argument like this isn't about proving your design is universally best — it's about justifying why it's the best choice given the specific criteria and constraints (the weighting) you were assigned. A design that wins under one weighting can lose under another; that's expected, not a flaw in your reasoning. What matters is whether your argument actually deals with the tradeoff, using your own real numbers from your design process, rather than leaning on one metric alone.
