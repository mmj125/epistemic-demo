Modeling a Field's Crops and Nitrogen

Real weather, real soil, and a real field model, computed in your browser. Each step below changes one thing at a time so you can tell what's actually driving what you see — the last step opens the whole model up for you to investigate on your own.

Season Progress

Before comparing anything, get familiar with what the model actually tracks day by day. Pick a crop and a weather year below and run it — watch canopy cover rise as the plant grows, water stress rise and fall with the weather, and biomass accumulate underneath both. Every later tab in this investigation compares outcomes like these; this tab is where you find out what those words mean.

Canopy cover is the fraction of ground shaded by the crop, from bare soil (0%) to full cover. Water stress runs from 0 (no stress) to 1 (severely stressed) — low soil moisture on a hot day pushes it up, and it holds the plant's growth back on days it's high. Biomass is the plant material accumulated so far, the running total that canopy cover and water stress together determine day by day.

Try a dry year against a wet one (2012 was a real drought at this site; 1993 was wet) and watch how the water-stress line changes shape, not just its average.

Picking a real field of your own

The two comparisons above use fixed sites so there's a known answer to predict. Later in this investigation (Open Investigation, the last tab) you'll run this same model against a real field of your own choosing, anywhere in the country. Learn that skill now, on something low-stakes, before it's needed for real.

Recommended: Google Earth Pro (free, desktop).
  1. Download and open Google Earth Pro, then search for your area of interest.
  2. Use the "Add Placemark" (a pin) or "Add Polygon" tool to mark your field.
  3. In the "Places" panel on the left, right-click your new placemark or polygon and choose "Save Place As...".
  4. In the save dialog, set "Save as type" to Kml (*.kml) — not Kmz (*.kmz), which is a compressed version this tool can't read directly.
  5. Save the file, then upload it below to see it work.
Alternative: Google Earth Web (earth.google.com). Draw a placemark or shape, save it to a project, then use the project's "Export as KML file" option. One real catch: that option can sometimes still produce a .kmz file despite its name. If the file you download ends in .kmz, either look for a plain-KML export option or switch to Google Earth Pro above — this tool will tell you plainly if a .kmz slips through, rather than failing silently.

This upload here is just practice — it doesn't carry forward to the Open Investigation tab, which has its own upload step. Try a real place you know (your hometown, a local farm, anywhere) and confirm the tool finds real soil and weather data for it.

Crop Choice

Corn and soybean at the same site and year, but the story isn't as simple as "one crop always wins." Which one yields more depends on what else you're doing — specifically, how much nitrogen you're applying.

Tillage Practice

Same site, same weather year, same crop — only the tillage practice changes, across the same five real implements already built into the engine (no tillage, chisel plow, field cultivator, disk, moldboard plow, ordered by their real soil-disturbance intensity, not by name). Two real, already-verified mechanisms drive what you'll see: tillage genuinely mixes soil moisture within the implement's own real depth, and it temporarily boosts background soil nitrogen, sized by the implement's own real mixing-efficiency number, not a guess. Predict where the five practices actually spread apart: at a low nitrogen rate, or a high one.

Nitrogen Rate

Fertilizer nitrogen a crop doesn't use has to end up somewhere. This tab follows it: applied nitrogen sits in a soil pool, the crop draws from that pool as it grows, and whatever's left when water drains past the root zone washes out with it. Raise the rate and watch how much larger the unused share gets; switch between a dry year and a wet one, or a sandy soil and a clay-rich one, to see that the split isn't fixed by the nitrogen rate alone.

Rotations and Cover Crops

A corn → winter cover crop → soybean sequence, evaluated on nitrogen leaching rather than just yield — the direct tie to the Chesapeake Bay nutrient-runoff framing this unit keeps coming back to. Brings the nitrogen and tillage controls from earlier tabs into one realistic multi-phase scenario, and shows a cover crop measurably cutting how much nitrogen reaches the watershed compared to the same field left bare over the same window.

Open Investigation

Then run this model against it under different conditions (crop, nitrogen rate, tillage, weather year), changing one lever at a time and logging what happens. Once you've logged at least two runs, see what a farmer maximizing profit and a water-quality advocate minimizing runoff would each choose for your field, then write your own final recommendation as the policymaker who has to weigh both — citing specific runs from your own log, not just a preference.
