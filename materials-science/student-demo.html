epiSTEMic/Outliers and honest claims
For teachers · pre-brief

Lesson purpose

Students use a real, right-skewed data set to locate a percentile, compare inclusion rules, and evaluate how outlier handling changes a claim.

The central question is ethical as well as computational: when is removing a measurement defensible, and when does it become cherry-picking?

Before students begin

Students should know median and ordered data. The page computes quartiles using the CCSS glossary convention: medians of the lower and upper halves.

The 1.5 × IQR cutoff is presented as a flagging convention, not a command to delete data. Plan about 25–35 minutes plus discussion.

Listen for this misconception

“Outliers are mistakes, so you should remove them.” An outlier is a reason to look closer, not a reason to delete.

The situation

A lab wants to build a switch only a few atoms thick.

Computer chips contain billions of tiny switches called transistors. This lab tests atom-thin layers. Bumpy surfaces can make those switches work poorly.

How the microscope works

It feels the surface instead of looking at it.

An atomic force microscope drags a very sharp tip across the film, like a record-player needle. It records the height at each spot.

What the microscope sees

Two real surfaces, measured this way.

Each image is thousands of recorded heights. Roughness is the typical bump height, in nanometres. One nanometre is a few atoms wide.

For teachers · debrief and discussion

Key discussion

Report A removed rough scans because they were rough. Its summary describes the smoother remainder, not the lab’s recorded scans.

A defensible rule fits the question, uses measurement evidence, is chosen before results, applies to every point, and is reported openly.

Ask: “Would you write this rule before collecting data?” and “Would it still seem fair if it made the result worse?”

Mathematical takeaways

The mean reacts strongly to the long right tail. The median and IQR are more resistant, while Q3 answers the 75th-percentile question.

The 1.5 × IQR fence flags unusually distant observations. It does not prove error and does not automatically justify exclusion.

A different kind of correction

In one WSe₂ scan, one corrupted line out of 512 raised roughness from 0.80 nm to 6.10 nm.

Correcting that documented instrument fault, while keeping both values, differs from deleting an entire scan because it is rough.
